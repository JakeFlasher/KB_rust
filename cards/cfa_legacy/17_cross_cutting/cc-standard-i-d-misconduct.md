# Standard I(D) Misconduct

## Intuition

Standard I.D PROHIBITS Members and Candidates from engaging in any
professional conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, or deceit OR from
committing any act that reflects adversely on their professional
reputation, integrity, or competence. The Standard has two
conceptually distinct prongs: (1) the professional-conduct prong
(any dishonest professional act); (2) the residual-reputation prong
(any personal act that reflects adversely on professional standing).
**Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.340-342.

The residual-reputation prong is the cleanup rule for conduct that
would otherwise escape the more specific Standards: a Member who
embezzles from a charity is captured by I.D even though no client,
employer, or capital-market participant is directly involved.
However, the Standard carves out private morality absent a
professional nexus — a Member's lawful personal lifestyle choices
(political views, religious practice, recreational activities) are
NOT I.D violations unless they bear on professional integrity.
**Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.340-341.

## Definition

Standard I.D PROHIBITS Members and Candidates from engaging in any
professional conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, or deceit OR from
committing any act that reflects adversely on their professional
reputation, integrity, or competence. **Source:** CFA Institute
(2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.340-341.

The professional-conduct prong reaches any dishonest act undertaken
in the course of professional activities — fraud against a client,
deceit toward an employer or regulator, or any act that would
violate the more specific I.C / II.A / III.D Standards from a
fraud / deceit angle. The Standards' Guidance notes that I.D is
often charged alongside the more specific Standard when a single
fact pattern violates both. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1
Vol.6/pp.340-341.

The residual-reputation prong reaches any act — whether
professional or personal — that reasonably could be expected to
reflect adversely on professional reputation, integrity, or
competence. The Standard's Guidance identifies lying, cheating, or
stealing in business or personal life, fraudulent or deceitful
circumstances surrounding a personal bankruptcy, alcohol abuse
during business hours or intoxication while making investment
decisions, and the civil-disobedience boundary (lawful personal
advocacy distinguished from conduct that reflects adversely on
professional integrity) as the canonical violation fact-patterns.
**Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.341-342.

The Recommended Procedures for I.D (RECOMMENDS) include adopting a
written firm code of ethics that all employees acknowledge,
maintaining a list of violation categories and corresponding
disciplinary sanctions, and conducting employment-history /
reference checks on prospective hires to surface professional-
integrity flags before the hire is made. **Source:** CFA Institute
(2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.341-342.

## Mathematical Reasoning

The two-prong structure of I.D (source ASSERTS) maps the conduct
space into a quadrant: (Prong-1, professional) catches dishonest
acts at work; (Prong-2, personal) catches private acts that reflect
on professional standing. The Standard does NOT police private
morality absent a professional nexus — this is the most
exam-tested distinction in I.D. The bright-line test the
Standards apply is whether the reasonable investor or client would
lose confidence in the Member's professional competence or
integrity upon learning of the conduct. Lawful personal-lifestyle
choices that the reasonable observer would NOT find professionally
relevant fall outside I.D's reach. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022)
L1 Vol.6/pp.340-342.

The overlap with more specific Standards (source ASSERTS) is a
deliberate redundancy: a charge of insider trading violates II.A
directly, but the PCP may also charge I.D's professional-conduct
prong on the theory that insider trading is fraud-on-the-market and
therefore inherently dishonest. The redundancy gives the PCP
prosecutorial flexibility and ensures no conduct gap. The general
rule for exam purposes is to identify the MOST specific Standard
violated (e.g., II.A for insider trading; III.A for fiduciary
breach; IV.A for employer disloyalty) and only invoke I.D when no
more specific Standard fits or when the misconduct is on the
residual-reputation prong (personal-conduct fraud). **Source:** CFA
Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.341-342.

The exam-cheating fact pattern (source REQUIRES) violates BOTH I.D
and VII.A: I.D because exam dishonesty reflects on professional
integrity, and VII.A because conduct compromises the integrity of
CFA Institute programs. The Standards-Application materials use this
double-charge fact pattern to illustrate how multi-Standard
violations are typically pleaded by the PCP. **Source:** CFA
Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.341-342.
