# Aggregate Demand and the Representative Consumer

## Intuition

Macro models routinely write aggregate demand as `Y^D = C + I + G + (X − M)` or treat aggregate consumption as a function of aggregate income `C(Y)` — as if the entire economy were one consumer. This shortcut is the **representative-consumer assumption**, and it is not automatic: it requires the heterogeneous individual demand functions to aggregate cleanly into a single demand function. MWG Ch.4 asks when this is possible and shows that the answer depends on the structure of individual preferences. Three sufficient conditions: (a) all consumers have **identical homothetic preferences** (then aggregate demand depends only on aggregate wealth, not on its distribution); (b) preferences exhibit **Gorman polar form** (allows different consumers but with the same slope of Engel curves); (c) **quasi-linear preferences** (numeraire good absorbs all income effects). When none of these holds, distribution effects make aggregate demand depend on individual wealth distribution, not just the total. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.105-126.

```
   individual demand          aggregate demand
   ─────────────────          ────────────────
   x_i(p, w_i) for each i      X(p, W) = Σ x_i(p, w_i)
                                  │
                                  │ depends on distribution {w_1, ..., w_I}
                                  │ unless aggregation condition holds
                                  ▼
   if preferences satisfy:        X(p, W) depends only on W = Σ w_i
   - Gorman form, OR              and is the demand of a single
   - identical homothetic, OR     representative consumer with wealth W
   - quasi-linear

   otherwise: distribution matters; representative-consumer fails
```

This card explains the conditions under which the macroeconomic representative-consumer shortcut is valid. The CFA L1 AS-AD framework (sibling `ec-aggregate-supply-demand-mechanics`) and the Solow / Ramsey / RBC frameworks (Batch 1 macro cards) all assume the conditions hold and use a single aggregate demand function `Y^D(p)`; this card states the foundation so the macro framings can stand on it. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.110-126.

## Definition

A consumer's preferences are **homothetic** if her preferences over consumption bundles are invariant under proportional scaling: `x ≿ y ⇔ λx ≿ λy` for all `λ > 0`. Equivalently, her utility function can be normalized to homogeneous of degree 1: `u(λx) = λ u(x)`. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.108-112.

A consumer's preferences are **quasi-linear** in good 1 (numeraire) if her utility takes the form `u(x_1, x_2, ..., x_L) = x_1 + φ(x_2, ..., x_L)` for some function `φ`. Income effects are absorbed entirely by good 1; demand for goods `2, ..., L` is independent of wealth. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.112-115.

A family of indirect utility functions `{v_i(p, w_i)}_{i=1}^I` has the **Gorman polar form** if each can be written as `v_i(p, w_i) = a_i(p) + b(p) · w_i` for functions `a_i(p)` (consumer-specific intercept) and `b(p)` (common slope in wealth). The slope `b(p)` is the same across all consumers; the intercept may differ. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.115-122.

**Aggregate demand** is `X(p, w_1, ..., w_I) = Σ_i x_i(p, w_i)` where `x_i(p, w_i)` is consumer `i`'s Walrasian demand. The aggregate demand **depends only on aggregate wealth** `W = Σ w_i` (and not on the distribution `{w_1, ..., w_I}`) if there exists a function `X̃(p, W)` such that `X(p, w_1, ..., w_I) = X̃(p, W)` for any redistribution preserving the total. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.105-122.

**Representative-consumer property**: a representative consumer with utility function `u_R(x)` exists if aggregate demand `X̃(p, W)` is the Walrasian demand of `u_R(x)` at prices `p` and wealth `W`. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.122-126.

## Mathematical Reasoning

**Gorman aggregation theorem** (Proposition 4.B.1 in MWG): aggregate demand `X(p, w_1, ..., w_I)` depends only on aggregate wealth `W = Σ w_i` (for any distribution preserving `W`) if and only if each consumer's indirect utility takes the Gorman polar form `v_i(p, w_i) = a_i(p) + b(p) · w_i` with the **same** `b(p)` across all `i`. The intuition: the common slope `b(p)` is the consumer-invariant marginal utility of wealth, so redistribution among consumers leaves each consumer's marginal valuation of any good unchanged, hence the substitution patterns and the aggregate consumption are invariant to redistribution. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.115-122.

**Sufficient conditions for Gorman**: . **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) pp.105-126.
- **Identical homothetic preferences**: when all consumers have the same homothetic utility, their indirect utility functions satisfy the Gorman form with `a_i(p) = 0` (constant) and `b(p) = 1/e(p, 1)` (the inverse of the expenditure for unit utility). Aggregate demand is then identical to the demand of a single consumer with wealth `W` and the same homothetic utility. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.118-121.
- **Quasi-linear preferences**: when all consumers have quasi-linear utility in the same numeraire (with potentially different `φ_i` for goods `2, ..., L`), each `v_i(p, w_i) = w_i + ã_i(p)` satisfies Gorman with `b(p) = 1`. Demand for goods `2, ..., L` is independent of wealth distribution. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.118-121.
- **Linear Engel curves with same slope**: a slightly more general condition than identical homothetic; allows different intercepts (consumer-specific autonomous consumption) but requires the same marginal propensity to consume each good out of wealth. Gorman polar form is the formal statement. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.115-122.

When **none of these conditions hold**, redistribution among consumers can shift aggregate demand even at fixed total wealth. A textbook counterexample: consumer A has a strong taste for good 1, consumer B has a strong taste for good 2; transferring wealth from A to B shifts aggregate demand toward good 2 even though aggregate wealth is unchanged. The representative-consumer fiction breaks; one must use individual-level demand functions and aggregate explicitly. Modern heterogeneous-agent macro (HANK) models reject the representative-consumer shortcut on exactly this ground for distributional / inequality questions. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.122-126.

**Implication for the macro cards in Batch 1**: the Solow model, the Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model, and the New Keynesian DSGE framework all assume a representative consumer (either identical homothetic preferences across an infinite-horizon dynasty, or quasi-linear preferences in the numeraire). The validity of those macro analyses hinges on the conditions stated here. The CFA L1 AS-AD framework (sibling `ec-aggregate-supply-demand-mechanics`) takes the representative-consumer shortcut without explicit foundation; this card supplies the foundation so the L1-core card can stand on it. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.122-126.

### Gorman vs Identical Homothetic — When Each Holds

The two sufficient conditions for representative-consumer aggregation cover different empirical scenarios. **Identical homothetic preferences** assume every consumer has the same utility function (so the same Engel curve), making distribution irrelevant by symmetry. This is the strongest sufficient condition; it works for any homothetic specification (Cobb-Douglas, CES, Stone-Geary with zero subsistence level), but fails empirically because consumers do exhibit different preferences over goods. **Gorman polar form** is more general: consumers may have different intercepts in their Engel curves (different "autonomous consumption") but must share the same **slope** (same marginal propensity to consume each good out of wealth). The Gorman family includes identical homothetic as a special case (zero intercept differences). Quasi-linear preferences are another Gorman special case (zero slope for the non-numeraire goods). **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.115-122.

## Boundary Notes

The representative-consumer construct is a **modeling convenience**, not a behavioral claim about the existence of a single "average" consumer in the economy. When the Gorman / homothetic / quasi-linear conditions hold, the macro aggregate behaves **as if** a single rational consumer were maximizing utility subject to aggregate wealth; when those conditions fail, the macro aggregate exhibits distribution-dependent properties (e.g., aggregate marginal propensity to consume varies with wealth inequality even at fixed total wealth) that no single-agent utility function can represent. The validity of the representative-consumer shortcut therefore varies by question: it is reliable for first-order aggregate-output questions under standard preferences and unreliable for distribution-sensitive questions like wealth inequality, fiscal redistribution effects, or financial-frictions transmission. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.122-126.

The CFA L1 AS-AD framework (`ec-aggregate-supply-demand-mechanics`) and the Solow / Ramsey / Romer growth models (Batch 1 macro cards) all take the representative-consumer shortcut **without explicit foundation**. This card supplies the foundation so the L1-core card and Batch 1 macro cards can stand on it. The shortcut's validity for those cards' questions (aggregate output, growth, inflation-unemployment tradeoff) is in the "first-order" category where the representative consumer is reliable. Heterogeneous-agent extensions (HANK, TANK) that explicitly relax the representative-consumer shortcut for distributional questions are out of v10 scope and live in advanced macro courses. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.122-126.

### Wealth-Distribution Robustness Tests

A pragmatic test for whether aggregate macro analysis can use the representative-consumer shortcut is to ask: does the policy question turn on **wealth-distribution effects** or only on **aggregate-wealth effects**? Aggregate-output growth (Solow), business-cycle propagation (RBC), and Phillips-curve mechanics primarily turn on aggregate-wealth effects and tolerate the shortcut. Fiscal-redistribution analysis, financial-friction transmission, and inequality dynamics turn on distributional effects and require heterogeneous-agent extensions. The 02 Batch 1 macro cards stay within the aggregate-effects category by construction; the L1-core AS-AD card (`ec-aggregate-supply-demand-mechanics`) likewise uses aggregate `Y^D = C + I + G + (X − M)` without distribution disaggregation. **Source:** Mas-Colell et al. (1995) Ch.4 pp.122-126.
