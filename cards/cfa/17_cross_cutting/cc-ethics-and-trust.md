# Ethics and Trust in the Investment Profession

## Intuition

Investment professionals stand between savers and the real economy as
agents allocating capital they do not own. The relationship is built
on an information asymmetry — the manager knows the portfolio, the
strategy, the fees, and the risks; the client typically does not.
Trust is the only mechanism that resolves this asymmetry at scale: a
client who cannot verify the manager's diligence in real time must
rely on the manager's professional ethic to do the work as if the
client were watching. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1
Vol.6/pp.255-265.

The CFA Institute frames ethics not as a private moral preference but
as a public good for capital markets. When trust holds, savers
willingly delegate capital, capital flows to productive uses, and the
entire intermediation function — banks, asset managers, brokers,
advisors — functions. When trust breaks, the principal-agent friction
forces clients to substitute monitoring (legal contracts, custodial
arrangements, performance-fee structures) for trust, and the
intermediation surplus shrinks. Individual ethics breaches therefore
have market-level externalities. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1
Vol.6/pp.265-280.

## Definition

Ethics in the investment profession is the body of conduct
expectations whose adherence supports the trust-based delegation of
capital from clients to managers. The CFA Institute defines this
framework as the Code of Ethics (six normative principles introduced
in the next sibling card) supplemented by Standards of Professional
Conduct (Standards I-VII, the binding rules elaborated in Batches 1
and 2). **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.270-275.

The framework distinguishes ethical from legal obligations: a conduct
may be lawful but unethical (e.g., aggressive but non-fraudulent
performance presentation), or unethical AND unlawful (e.g., insider
trading violating Standard II.A and securities law). The "more strict
of law or Standard" rule (Standard I.A) governs the cross-border
case where local law is permissive but the Standard is binding for
CFA-charter holders. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1
Vol.6/pp.267-272.

Members and Candidates voluntarily commit to the Code and Standards by
joining CFA Institute and by enrolling in the CFA Program; the
commitment is enforced by CFA Institute's Professional Conduct
Program, which can issue sanctions ranging from private censure
through summary suspension up to revocation of membership and
designation. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.275-284.

## Mathematical Reasoning

The structural argument for why ethics matters to markets (source
ASSERTS, not proves) goes: capital markets price assets efficiently
only if the information used to set prices is reliable; the
intermediaries who produce and disseminate that information
(analysts, managers, advisors) sit between asset issuers and asset
buyers; absent an enforced ethic, the intermediaries face a strict
incentive to bias information toward their own compensation
(higher fees, hidden conflicts, undisclosed inventory positions); the
resulting price distortion harms not only the immediate client but
all participants whose prices reflect the contaminated information.
The conclusion REQUIRES intermediaries to subordinate self-interest
to client-interest as a market-wide condition, not merely a
client-by-client one. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1
Vol.6/pp.260-270.

The professional-stature argument (source ASSERTS) recognizes that
the CFA designation is itself a trust signal: clients delegate to
charter-holders partly because the designation certifies adherence to
the Code and Standards. Ethics breaches by a single charter-holder
therefore impose a negative externality on every other charter-holder
by eroding the designation's signal value. The Code's six principles
operationalize this by REQUIRING charter-holders to act with
integrity, exercise diligent professional care, and place client
interests above firm and personal interests — all framed as
profession-wide obligations rather than client-by-client ones.
**Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1 Vol.6/pp.270-280.

The ethical-vs-legal distinction (source ASSERTS) splits the conduct
space into four quadrants: (a) lawful AND ethical (the target zone);
(b) lawful but unethical (the gap zone where the Standards bind even
absent legal liability — e.g., legal but undisclosed soft-dollar
arrangements violate VI.A); (c) unlawful but ethical (rare edge case
where the law is the bug, not the conduct — the Standards do not
PERMIT illegal conduct even if morally defensible); (d) unlawful
AND unethical (insider trading violating both II.A and securities
law). The Standards govern quadrants (a) and (b); the law governs
(c) and (d). Standard I.A's "more strict" rule resolves the
cross-border case where local-law permissiveness creates
quadrant-(b) opportunities. **Source:** CFA Institute (2022) L1
Vol.6/pp.267-273.
