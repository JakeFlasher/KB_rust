# Myopic Loss Aversion and the Equity Premium

## Intuition

Myopic loss aversion is the practitioner-relevant compound of two ingredients: *loss aversion* (a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases) and *myopia* (evaluating the portfolio too frequently and over too narrow a frame). Frequent evaluation matters because the more often a loss-averse investor checks the portfolio, the more often they confront a loss, and each loss is felt disproportionately — so equities, which fall often over short horizons even while compounding handsomely over long ones, feel far riskier than their long-run distribution warrants. Pompian's Chapter 19 Research Review states this resolution of the *equity-premium puzzle* directly: Benartzi and Thaler's "myopic loss aversion" answers why stocks have historically out-yielded T-bills (Mehra-Prescott's 6.18% premium) by a margin too large for plausible risk aversion — investors price equities as if their horizon were short (a simulation-implied evaluation period of about one year), demanding a large premium to bear the frequent paper losses, so myopic, loss-averse investors gravitate toward bonds.
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.212.

Pompian's 2006 edition supplies the loss-aversion building block directly. Loss aversion comes from Kahneman-Tversky's 1979 prospect theory: the possibility of a loss is on average twice as powerful a motivator as an equal-magnitude gain, so risks that do not "pay double" are unacceptable. The mechanism in practice is the disposition effect — holding losers too long (risk-seeking in the loss domain) and selling winners too soon (risk-averse in the gain domain) — and the trigger is the monthly statement: investors open the statement, see winners and losers, and react asymmetrically. Frequent statement review is the "myopia" channel in practitioner clothing.
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.209-210.

**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.212.

## Definition

**Loss aversion** (Kahneman-Tversky, 1979) is the emotional bias by which people feel a stronger impulse to avoid losses than to acquire equal-magnitude gains — a common rule of thumb being roughly a 2:1 weighting, so a risk must offer about a two-dollar gain per dollar at risk to be acceptable.
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.208.

**Reference point** is the benchmark (the origin in Figure 19.1) against which all gains and losses are weighed; the value function passing through it is asymmetric, steeper for losses than for gains.
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.209.

**Disposition effect** (Shefrin-Statman) is the joint tendency to hold losing investments too long (risk-seeking in the loss domain) and sell winning investments too quickly (risk-avoidance in the gain domain).
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.210.

**Myopic loss aversion** (Benartzi-Thaler, presented in Pompian's Ch.19 Research Review) is loss aversion combined with frequent evaluation / narrow framing, which inflates perceived equity risk and is offered as the solution to the equity-premium puzzle — investors who evaluate their portfolios most frequently are the most likely to experience losses and so gravitate to bonds, making an asset's value depend on the investor's time horizon.
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.212.

## Mathematical Reasoning

The loss-aversion ingredient is the asymmetric, reference-dependent value function `v` of prospect theory: defined over deviations `x` from the reference point, `v` is concave for gains (`x > 0`, risk-averse) and convex for losses (`x < 0`, risk-seeking), with a kink at the origin where the loss limb is steeper. The 2:1 rule of thumb is the loss-aversion coefficient `lambda ≈ 2` in `v(x) = x` for `x >= 0` and `v(x) = lambda * x` for `x < 0`; the inequality `|v(-d)| > v(+d)` for `d > 0` is the formal content of "a loss is twice as powerful." (This piecewise-linear `lambda ≈ 2` form is one common Tversky-Kahneman-style parameterization of Pompian's 2:1 rule of thumb, not Pompian's own equation: Pompian states the 2:1 motivator ratio and draws the S-shape qualitatively but writes no value-function equation.) Pompian draws exactly this S-shape in Figure 19.1 (gains right, losses left, value vertical) and attributes the disposition effect to the differing curvature on the two limbs.
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.209-210.

```
            Value v(x)
               |        ___---  gains: concave (risk-averse)
               |     _--
   ------------+--*-------------- x  (deviation from reference)
            _--  | <- kink at reference; loss limb steeper
         _-      |
  losses: convex |
  (risk-seeking) |
   Figure 19.1: S-shaped, loss-averse value function
```

The myopia ingredient is stated in Pompian's Research Review: under Benartzi-Thaler, the evaluation frequency `n` (how often the loss-averse investor "closes the books" and re-references) sets the effective horizon. Shorter evaluation periods raise `P(loss over the period)`, and since each loss is weighted by `lambda > 1`, the expected `v` of equities falls and the required premium rises; lengthening the evaluation horizon shrinks the demanded premium. Pompian reports the simulation-implied evaluation period consistent with the realized equity risk premium is about one year, and that nonmyopic (long-horizon) investors are more willing to hold risky assets — so an asset's value depends on the investor's time horizon. (The source narrates this mechanism and its one-year result; the full simulation calibration lives in the primary Benartzi-Thaler paper.)
**Source:** Pompian (2006) Ch.19 pp.212.
