# Ambiguity, the Ellsberg Paradox, and Sources

## Intuition

Ambiguity is uncertainty extending beyond risk: situations where probabilities are unknown rather than given. The Ellsberg paradox is the canonical demonstration. Offered a known urn (50/50 composition) and an unknown urn (unknown composition), most people prefer to bet on the known urn for *both* colors — a preference that cannot be reconciled with any single subjective probability, since betting against both colors of the unknown urn would require both color-probabilities to fall below 1/2. The paradox violates probabilistic sophistication, and a nonadditive capacity `W` accommodates it by setting `W` higher on the known-urn events than on the matching unknown-urn events.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §10.3 pp.286, §11.1 pp.317-318.

Wakker's framing is that ambiguity is fundamentally a *between-source* comparison within one person. A *source* is a set of events generated by the same mechanism (the known urn, the unknown urn, the Dow Jones, the Nikkei). The decision maker can be more pessimistic about one source than another, and this relative attitude — not an absolute deviation from objective probabilities — is what Ellsberg and the home bias reveal. Each source gets its own *source function*, a probability weighting curve whose shape encodes the ambiguity attitude (aversion as elevation/pessimism, insensitivity as curvature) toward that source's events.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §11.1 pp.317-318, §11.2 pp.320.

## Definition

**Source** is a specific set of uncertain events generated by the same mechanism; taken as a formal primitive (an "algebra" of events).
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §11.1 pp.318.

**Source preference** of source A over source B holds if, for all events `A` in A and `B` in B, `[A >= B and A^c > B^c]` may hold but `[B >= A and B^c > A^c]` is precluded — a systematic tilt toward betting on A's events even after correcting for likelihood beliefs.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §11.1 pp.318.

**Uniform source** is a set of events that intuitively have the same degree of ambiguity, so that probabilistic sophistication holds *within* the source via a probability measure `P_So`.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §11.2 pp.320.

**Source function** `w_So` is the probability weighting function for the source, with `W(E) = w_So(P_So(E))`; its shape reflects the uncertainty attitude toward that source. Filtering out the risk attitude `w` gives `w^{-1} w_So`, the pure ambiguity attitude.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §11.2 pp.320.

## Mathematical Reasoning

For an event `E` in a uniform source `So`, the rank-dependent weight factors through the source's probability and source function:

```
W(E) = w_So( P_So(E) ),
```

so the concepts developed for probability weighting under risk (likelihood insensitivity = curvature; pessimism/optimism = elevation) carry over directly to `w_So` to analyze ambiguity. Ambiguity attitude is the deviation of `w_So` from the risk weighting `w`; isolating it gives `w^{-1} w_So`. In the two-color Ellsberg example with symmetry `1_{B_a} 0 ~ 1_{R_a} 0`, one sets `P(B_a)=P(R_a)=1/2`, and the matching probability `w^{-1}(w_a(1/2))` reflects the ambiguity attitude toward the unknown urn — below 1/2 for both colors under ambiguity aversion. The index `1/2 - w^{-1}(w_a(1/2))` measures ambiguity aversion.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §11.2 pp.320-321.

The nonadditivity that drives Ellsberg is visible directly: in the accommodating capacity, `1 = W(S) = W(R_a ∪ B_a) != W(R_a) + W(B_a) = 0.35 + 0.35 = 0.70`, so `W` is subadditive (convex), capturing the aversion to the unknown urn relative to the known urn where `W(B_k)=W(R_k)=0.45`.
**Source:** Wakker (2010) §10.3 pp.286-287.

```
Ellsberg, two sources within one agent:
  known urn   :  W(B_k) = W(R_k) = 0.45   (moderate deviation from 1/2)
  unknown urn :  W(B_a) = W(R_a) = 0.35   (stronger deviation: ambiguity aversion)
  source preference: bet on known urn for BOTH colors  ->  W subadditive on unknown source
```
