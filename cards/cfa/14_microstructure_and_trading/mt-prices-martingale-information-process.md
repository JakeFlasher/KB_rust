# Prices as a Martingale and the Information-Impounding Price Process

## Intuition
In an information-based market the dealer does not know whether the trader on the other side knows something he does not. Competition forces the dealer to a zero-expected-profit, "regret-free" quote: the price at which he buys or sells is set equal to his *conditional expectation* of the asset's value, given exactly the information that the arriving trade reveals. Because each posted price is already the best forecast of value using everything currently known, there is no systematic gap left between today's price and the expectation of tomorrow's. That is precisely the martingale property — prices behave as a *fair game*. A bystander watching the tape can do no better at forecasting the next price than to quote the current one.

The deep point is that information enters prices *only through trades*. Each trade is a noisy signal; the dealer Bayes-updates his belief, and his revised belief becomes the new price. So the price sequence is simultaneously (i) a record of how the dealer has learned and (ii) a martingale with respect to his expanding information set. Over time, as more trades arrive, the true value gets impounded into price and beliefs converge to full information.

```
   nature picks V (low or high)         dealer's information set I_t grows by one trade each step
        |                                       |        |        |
        v                                       v        v        v
   trade_1 --> Bayes update --> p_1 = E[V|I_1]     p_2 = E[V|I_2]     p_3 = E[V|I_3] ...
                                  \________________  martingale: E[p_{t+1} | I_t] = p_t  ________________/
                                  information impounded gradually --> p_t > V  (convergence)
```

The martingale here is a property of the *equilibrium price path itself*, not just of an idealized fundamental value: whatever sequence of trades nature and the traders generate, the resulting price walk is a fair game.

**Source:** O'Hara (1995) ch.3 "Information-Based Models" pp.69-74

## Definition
Setup (a simplified competitive Glosten-Milgrom market):
- A single risky asset has terminal value `V`, taking a low value `V_L` or a high value `V_H`.
- A fraction `μ` of traders are *informed* (they know which value obtains); the remaining `1 − μ` are *uninformed* (liquidity) traders who buy or sell for exogenous reasons.
- Risk-neutral, competitive market makers observe the order flow and post bid `b_t` and ask `a_t`. Competition drives expected profit on each trade to zero.
- `I_t` denotes the market maker's information set at time `t` — the public history of trades up to `t`. `B_t` (`S_t`) denotes the event that the time-`t` trade is a buy (sell) from the market maker.

Regret-free (conditional-expectation) pricing: the ask is the expected value conditional on facing a buyer, and the bid is the expected value conditional on facing a seller,
```
a_t = E[V | B_t, I_{t-1}],     b_t = E[V | S_t, I_{t-1}].
```
A process `{p_t}` is a **martingale with respect to `{I_t}`** if `E[p_{t+1} | I_t] = p_t`. The transaction-price sequence generated by the conditional-expectation quotes above satisfies this property.

**Source:** O'Hara (1995) ch.3 §"Information-Based Models" pp.69-73

## Mathematical Reasoning
Why conditional-expectation pricing forces a martingale: by construction each transaction price is `p_t = E[V | I_t]`, where `I_t` is the market maker's information after observing the time-`t` trade. The information set grows monotonically, `I_t ⊆ I_{t+1}`. Applying the law of iterated expectations to the conditional expectation of the *next* price,
```
E[p_{t+1} | I_t] = E[ E[V | I_{t+1}] | I_t ] = E[V | I_t] = p_t,
```
since `I_t ⊆ I_{t+1}`. Hence `{p_t}` is a martingale with respect to the market maker's information filtration.

Immediate corollaries:
- **First differences are serially uncorrelated.** Write `Δp_{t+1} = p_{t+1} − p_t = ε_{t+1}`, the belief revision (the "innovation"). Because `ε_{t+1}` is unforecastable from `I_t` (`E[ε_{t+1} | I_t] = 0`) and successive innovations are orthogonal (`E[ε_t ε_s] = 0` for `s ≠ t`), the price-change autocovariance vanishes: `cov(Δp_t, Δp_s) = 0`. This contrasts with the *negative* serial correlation that pure inventory/transaction-cost spreads (Roll-type bid-ask bounce) induce — a wedge that lets one tell adverse-selection price impact apart from mechanical bounce.
- **Efficiency interpretation.** The martingale property means prices reflect all information available to the market maker, i.e. they are semi-strong-form efficient at every date; only in the limit, once trades have revealed `V`, do they attain strong-form efficiency. Along the way prices need not exhibit strong-form efficiency because some traders hold superior private information.
- **Information impounding / convergence.** Each trade shifts the posterior via Bayes' rule; the spread `a_t − b_t > 0` is the adverse-selection premium, decreasing as beliefs sharpen, with `p_t → V` as information is fully impounded.

No worked arithmetic is reproduced here; the algebra of the Bayes update and the closed-form spread is carried in the sibling Bayesian-learning card and in O'Hara's Appendix.

**Source:** O'Hara (1995) ch.3 pp.73-74; Foucault, Pagano & Röell (2013) §3 p.83

## Boundary Notes
- **Assumptions that make the martingale hold.** Risk-neutral *and* competitive market makers (zero expected profit), commonly observed order flow, and *no inventory effects*. O'Hara is explicit that with risk aversion, capital constraints, or order flow not visible to all dealers, prices may reflect idiosyncratic inventory positions and the clean conditional-expectation/martingale characterization breaks; transaction-cost or inventory pricing instead injects negative serial correlation into price changes.
- **Martingale ≠ Markov.** O'Hara stresses these prices are martingales but not Markov: the full trade history matters for the posterior, so knowing only the current price is insufficient to reconstruct the dealer's beliefs.
- **Efficiency caveat in a dynamic setting.** Many distinct price-adjustment paths are all martingales yet differ in how close they sit to true value, so "efficiency" is a weaker statement here than it sounds (the Black (1989) / Easley-O'Hara point).
- **Contrast with siblings.** This card is the *equilibrium-pricing* statement; `mt-random-walk-efficient-price` poses the martingale as a latent fundamental contaminated by transient bounce, and `mt-permanent-vs-transitory-price-components` decomposes observed price into the permanent (martingale) innovation plus a stationary transitory part.
